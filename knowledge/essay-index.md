## 1. Startup ideas: what to build

- ★ **How to Get Startup Ideas** (Nov 2012) `startupideas` — Don't brainstorm ideas; notice problems, ideally your own. The best ideas are ones the founders want, can build, and few others see as worth doing. Live in the future and build what's missing. A small group that wants it urgently (a well) beats a big group that wants it a little. Watch for made-up "sitcom" ideas, the tarpit, and the schlep and unsexy filters.
- ★ **Organic Startup Ideas** (Apr 2010) `organic` — Ask "what do you wish someone would make for you?" Ideas that grow out of your own life beat ideas you work out from afar for users you don't know.
- ★ **Schlep Blindness** (Jan 2012) `schlep` — Great ideas hide behind tedious, unpleasant work that everyone flinches from without noticing (Stripe's payments). Ask what would be valuable if it weren't such a pain.
- ★ **Frighteningly Ambitious Startup Ideas** (Mar 2012) `ambitious` — The biggest ideas (a new search engine, replacing email, ending Hollywood, curing disease) are scary, so people look away. Approach them sideways and start with a small piece.
- **Ideas for Startups** (Oct 2005) `ideas` — Ideas are cheap; execution is everything. Good ideas often come from questions like: what annoys people, what can be made cheaper, what used to be hard and is now easy. Start with the idea, then keep changing it.
- ★ **Why Smart People Have Bad Ideas** (Apr 2005) `bronze` — Smart hackers pick ideas because they're cool or because they thought of them first, not because they're likely to make money. Combining technology with the business side is learnable and powerful, like alloying copper and tin into bronze.
- **Crazy New Ideas** (May 2021) `newideas` — When a reasonable expert proposes something that sounds crazy, don't dismiss it. Big things start that way.
- **How to Get New Ideas** (Jan 2023) `getideas` — Notice anomalies: what seems strange, missing, or broken. The best place to look is the frontier of a field, where the gaps look surprisingly obvious.
- ★ **What Microsoft Is this the Altair Basic of?** (Feb 2015) `altair` — Almost every great company looked lame at first. A useful exercise is to explain why a successful company wasn't as lame as it seemed, and then ask the same of your own idea.
- ★ **Making Startups Powerful** (Sep 2026) `powerful` — Ask "what would make this company more powerful?", not just "how could it make more money?" Owning the customer relationship, having money (or tokens) flow through you, platforms and APIs, network effects, going full stack, a side feature that turns out to be the real business, helping users make money, playing the long game, generosity, selling to customers early in their life (Stripe), customers who decide fast, getting the data upstream (Rippling), staying out of "mafia" markets, and "just stand up straight." The constraint: it has to make things better for the customer.
- **The Brand Age** (Mar 2026) `brandage` — Brand is what's left when real differences between products disappear, and technology tends to erase those differences (Swiss watches after quartz). Branding pulls against good design. Don't build a business on selling brand. Follow interesting problems and you'll tend to end up in a field's golden age.
- **How to Start Google** (Mar 2024) `google` — Advice for 13-year-olds: get good at some technology, start your own projects, and find cofounders by working with people. Google started as a project that wasn't meant to be a company.
- **The Hardware Renaissance** (Oct 2012) `hw` — Hardware startups were doing well and the barriers to them were falling. Don't rule out an idea just because it involves atoms.
- **Tablets** (Dec 2010) `tablets` — Names a new category before the world had a word for it. Watch where computing is moving.
- **The Future of Web Startups** (Oct 2007) `webstartups` — Startups keep getting cheaper to start, which means more of them, younger founders, and more reasons for acquirers to buy them.
- **Web 2.0** (Nov 2005) `web20` — Underneath the hype the term pointed at real things: Ajax, user-generated content ("democracy"), and not abusing users. Make the web the way it ought to be.
- **Why Twitter is a Big Deal** (Apr 2009) `twitter` — Twitter was a new messaging protocol in which you don't specify recipients. New protocols that catch on are rare and therefore big.
- **The Trouble with the Segway** (Jul 2009) `segway` — People won't use a product that makes them look like dorks. Social cost is a real adoption barrier.
- **Why TV Lost** (Mar 2009) `convergence` — TV and computers didn't converge; computers replaced TV. Open platforms beat controlled ones, and incumbents are blinded by their business models.
- **Post-Medium Publishing** (Sep 2009) `publishing` — People never paid for content; they paid for the physical medium. When the medium goes, publishers need a new thing to sell.
- **The Acceleration of Addictiveness** (Jul 2010) `addiction` — Technological progress makes things more addictive, and that is speeding up. Build, and live, with this in mind.
- **The Other Road Ahead** (Sep 2001) `road` — The Viaweb thesis: web-based software beats desktop software. It allows constant releases, users don't install anything, and you see bugs right away. A huge opportunity for small, fast teams.
- **Microsoft is Dead** (Apr 2007) `microsoft` — Incumbents can be dead long before it shows. Google, the web, broadband, and Macs had made Microsoft irrelevant to new startups.
- **Apple's Mistake** (Nov 2009) `apple` — A broken App Store approval process alienated the programmers who build a platform's value. Don't mistreat your developers.
- **What Happened to Yahoo** (Aug 2010) `yahoo` — Easy money from ad revenue, and seeing itself as a media company instead of a technology company, kept Yahoo from caring about good programmers and building great products.
- **Return of the Mac** (Mar 2005) `mac` — The best hackers were switching to Macs. Where the best hackers go is a leading indicator.

## 2. Founders: who succeeds

- ★ **What We Look for in Founders** (Oct 2010) `founders` — In order: determination, flexibility, imagination, naughtiness, and friendship (strong cofounder bonds).
- ★ **The Anatomy of Determination** (Sep 2009) `determination` — Determination is the strongest predictor of success. It is made of willfulness plus discipline, and ambition makes it go further.
- ★ **Relentlessly Resourceful** (Mar 2009) `relres` — The best founders are relentlessly resourceful. They make things happen and aren't pushed around by circumstances. The opposite is hapless.
- **A Word to the Resourceful** (Jan 2012) `word` — The least successful startups were hard to talk to; advice didn't seem to get through. Being easy to talk to is a sign of being resourceful.
- ★ **The Right Kind of Stubborn** (Jul 2024) `persistence` — The persistent are attached to the goal; the obstinate are attached to their ideas about how to reach it. Persistence is made of energy, imagination, resilience, good judgment, and a goal. Listen to criticism closely. Change your mind lower in the decision tree and hold firm at the top.
- ★ **Earnestness** (Dec 2020) `earnest` — The highest compliment is "earnest": doing it for real reasons, intellectually honest, not cool or cynical. Formidable and earnest together is close to unstoppable.
- **The Airbnbs** (Dec 2020) `airbnbs` — Airbnb's founders were earnest and did nothing halfway. Cereal boxes, doing whatever it took, close attention to users.
- **The Reddits** (Mar 2024) `reddits` — PG rejected their first idea but funded them anyway, steering them to Reddit. Great companies match their founders' personalities. Launch fast; a v1 is tiny.
- ★ **Fierce Nerds** (May 2021) `fn` — Competitive nerds are formidable. Their independent-mindedness is a strength; their danger is the shoving-match side of competitiveness.
- ★ **Mean People Fail** (Nov 2014) `mean` — The most successful people are rarely mean. Meanness makes you stupid and drives away good people. In a world where ideas count, it doesn't pay.
- **Why It's Safe for Founders to Be Nice** (Aug 2015) `safe` — Startups win by making things people want, not by force, so being good isn't a handicap. Being nice also helps with hiring and partnerships.
- **The Ronco Principle** (Jan 2015) `ronco` — Ron Conway is both the most connected investor and genuinely good. Goodness compounds in a network business.
- ★ **Founder Mode** (Sep 2024) `foundermode` — Conventional "hire good people and give them room" advice fails founders. Founders should stay deeply engaged, skip levels, and run the company differently from professional managers.
- **Billionaires Build** (Dec 2020) `ace` — How to ace a YC interview is the same as how big fortunes are made now: explain what you're building and why users want it. The most successful founders are obsessed with building, not with getting rich.
- **Five Founders** (Apr 2009) `5founders` — Jobs, TJ Rodgers, Paul Buchheit, Larry & Sergey, and Sam Altman as models: taste and conviction, resolve, contrarian product sense, technical vision, and sheer force of will.
- **Jessica Livingston** (Nov 2015) `jessica` — Much of YC came from Jessica: judging people's character, spotting fakes, building community. Judgment of people is a crucial and underrated skill.
- **Charisma / Power** (Jan 2017) `pow` — Builders with power but little charisma become targets. That's a cost, not a reason to stop being a builder.
- **What Kate Saw in Silicon Valley** (Aug 2009) `kate` — An outsider's view: in the Valley, people are surprisingly nice, surprisingly young, and surprisingly ambitious, and trying is respected.
- ★ **Before the Startup** (Oct 2014) `before` — Startups are counterintuitive. You can't fake your way through them, and they take over your life. For students: don't start one just to start one; instead learn powerful things and work on projects.
- ★ **Why to Not Not Start a Startup** (Mar 2007) `notnot` — The usual reasons for not starting (too young, inexperienced, no idea, no cofounder) are mostly weaker than they seem. The real requirement is determination.
- **A Student's Guide to Startups** (Oct 2006) `mit` — Students can start startups. Cofounders are the critical ingredient and college is the best place to find them. Work on projects. The cost of trying is low.
- **Learning from Founders** (Jan 2007) `foundersatwork` — Foreword to *Founders at Work*. Startups are most productive at the very beginning; the winners slow down the least. Founders' stories show that luck, determination, and making things people want matter more than plans.
- **Hiring is Obsolete** (May 2005) `hiring` — Instead of applying for a job, start a startup and get bought. Acquisitions are a form of hiring where the market judges your work.
- **You Weren't Meant to Have a Boss** (Mar 2008) `boss` — Working in large organizations is unnatural and wears you down. Small groups of people choosing their own work is how humans thrive.
- **After the Ladder** (Aug 2005) `ladder` — Instead of climbing a corporate ladder, build the product yourself in a startup and sell it to the big company.
- **How Universities Should Prepare Founders** (Aug 2026) `prepare` — YC looks for people who are good at building things and have a habit of doing it. Study real subjects (CS, engineering, biology) and work on your own projects. Projects are where cofounders and the best ideas come from. Business-plan competitions mislead people: impress users with prototypes, not investors with stories.

## 3. Growth, execution, and survival

- ★ **Startup = Growth** (Sep 2012) `growth` — A startup is a company designed to grow fast, and everything else follows from that. Measure weekly growth on one key number; 5–7% a week is good and 10% is exceptional. Use the growth rate to make decisions.
- ★ **Do Things that Don't Scale** (Jul 2013) `ds` — Recruit users by hand, one at a time. Delight them obsessively. Do things manually before automating. Pick a narrow initial market. Consult for a first user and make the product fit them. Startups take off because the founders make them take off.
- ★ **Default Alive or Default Dead?** (Oct 2015) `aord` — With constant expenses and the current revenue growth rate, do you reach profitability before the money runs out? Most founders don't know. Find out now, and don't count on raising more.
- ★ **The Fatal Pinch** (Dec 2014) `pinch` — High burn, six months of runway, flat growth, and a plan to raise more is a trap. Investors can smell it. Cut costs and get to profitability.
- ★ **Ramen Profitable** (Jul 2009) `ramenprofitable` — Make just enough to cover the founders' living costs. It changes your relationship with investors and keeps your morale up.
- ★ **How Not to Die** (Aug 2007) `die` — Startups rarely die of competition; founders give up. If you don't die, you will probably succeed. The usual killers are cofounder fights, distraction, and running out of money.
- ★ **The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups** (Oct 2006) `startupmistakes` — Single founder, bad location, marginal niche, derivative idea, obstinacy, hiring bad programmers, choosing the wrong platform, slowness to launch, launching too early, having no specific user in mind, raising too little, spending too much, raising too much, poor investor management, sacrificing users to (supposed) profit, not wanting to get your hands dirty, fights between founders, a half-hearted effort.
- ★ **The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn** (Apr 2006) `startuplessons` — Release early. Keep pumping out features. Make users happy. Fear the right things (bad products, not competitors). Commitment is a self-fulfilling prophecy. There's always room. Don't get your hopes up.
- ★ **Startups in 13 Sentences** (Feb 2009) `13sentences` — Pick good cofounders. Launch fast. Let your idea evolve. Understand your users. Make a few users love you. Offer surprisingly good customer service. You make what you measure. Spend little. Get ramen profitable. Avoid distractions. Don't get demoralized. Don't give up. Deals fall through.
- ★ **How to Start a Startup** (Mar 2005) `start` — You need good people, something customers actually want, and as little spending as possible. Hire carefully, launch fast, stay cheap.
- ★ **Be Good** (Apr 2008) `good` — "Make something people want" is YC's motto. Don't worry much about the business model early. Act like a charity: helping people makes everything easier, from users to press to hiring.
- ★ **What I've Learned from Users** (Sep 2022) `users` — Most startups have the same problems. Founders are bad at ranking which problem matters most; fundraising trouble usually means the company is doing badly. Founders don't listen because startups are counterintuitive. Speed defines startups; focus enables speed. Correct course often: decisive at small scale, tentative at large scale.
- ★ **How to Earn a Billion Dollars** (Jun 2026) `earn` — Two numbers decide outcomes: the growth rate and how long it continues. At 15% a month for 5 years, revenue grows about 4,400x. You get the rate by making something people tell their friends about, and the duration by being in a big market. The key is empathy for users, not exploitation.
- **What Startups Are Really Like** (Oct 2009) `really` — From founders: cofounders matter enormously, startups take over your life, it's an emotional roller coaster, persistence matters, you can't predict what will work, deals fall through, investors are unpredictable, and users won't notice you at first.
- **Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule** (Jul 2009) `makersschedule` — Makers need half-day blocks of time; one meeting can ruin an afternoon. Guard the builders' time.
- **The Top Idea in Your Mind** (Jul 2010) `top` — What you think about in the shower is what you'll make progress on. Don't let money worries or disputes take that slot. Fundraising is dangerous partly because it does.
- **The Other Half of "Artists Ship"** (Nov 2008) `artistsship` — Big companies pile on checks to prevent mistakes, and that slows them down. Startups should accept more mistakes in return for speed.
- **Six Principles for Making New Things** (Feb 2008) `newthings` — Find simple solutions to overlooked problems that actually need solving, deliver them as informally as possible, start with a crude version, then iterate quickly.
- **Change Your Name** (Aug 2015) `name` — If you don't own the .com of your name, it signals weakness. Change the name.
- **What I've Learned from Hacker News** (Feb 2009) `hackernews` — Running a community: growth brings decline in quality; moderate early; the problem is less trolls than the mediocre and the mean.
- **Why to Start a Startup in a Bad Economy** (Oct 2008) `badeconomy` — The economy matters less than the founders. Downturns have advantages: cheaper talent, less competition, and lean habits.
- **How to Lose Time and Money** (Jul 2010) `selfindulgence` — Money is mostly lost through bad investments, and time through the modern, addictive versions of fake work and distraction. Watch out for things that feel like work but aren't.
- **What Business Can Learn from Open Source** (Aug 2005) `opensource` — Open source and blogging show that unpaid, bottom-up work can beat professional organizations. Office conditions and management layers hold back the best people.
- **Design and Research** (Jan 2003) `desres` — Design means making things good for users; research means making things new. Startups need design: start with a prototype and iterate with users.
- **Taste for Makers** (Feb 2002) `taste` — Good design is simple, timeless, solves the right problem, is suggestive, slightly funny, hard, looks easy, uses symmetry, resembles nature, redesigns, can copy, is strange, happens in chunks, and is daring.
- **Persuade xor Discover** (Sep 2009) `discover` — Writing that tries to persuade and writing that tries to find the truth pull in different directions. Pitches persuade; your thinking about the business should discover.

## 4. Fundraising and investors

- ★ **How to Raise Money** (Sep 2013) `fr` — Don't raise money unless you want it and it wants you. Be in fundraising mode or not. Get introductions, hear no until you hear yes, do breadth-first search weighted by expected value, know where you stand, get the first commitment, close committed money, avoid investors who don't lead, have multiple plans, underestimate how much you want, be nice, and the less you need the more you get. Dilution rule of thumb: under 15% in phase 1, at most 25% in phase 2; above about 40% sold in total, an A round gets harder.
- ★ **How to Convince Investors** (Aug 2013) `convince` — Convince with the truth, not with presentation. Investors look for formidable founders with a promising opportunity. Explain why the startup is worth investing in, clearly and honestly. Make it real by making progress.
- ★ **Investor Herd Dynamics** (Aug 2013) `herd` — Investors' opinions of you are driven by other investors' opinions. You can't manipulate that, but you can stop it from hurting you: close fast, and don't let a stalled round turn into a signal.
- ★ **A Fundraising Survival Guide** (Aug 2008) `fundraising` — Fundraising is brutal. Have low expectations, keep working on the company, stay flexible, avoid investors who hedge, accept offers greedily, and don't let fundraising stop you from working on the product.
- **The Hacker's Guide to Investors** (Apr 2007) `guidetoinvestors` — Investors are driven by fear of missing deals; few are smart about technology; they're slow and secretive; angels and VCs differ; the deal isn't done until the money is in the bank.
- **How to Present to Investors** (Aug 2006) `investors` — Explain what you're doing in one sentence. Focus on the problem, the solution, and why you'll win. Be concrete, use demos, and make it seem inevitable.
- **How to Fund a Startup** (Nov 2005) `startupfunding` — Funding works like gears: take only what you need to reach the next level. Covers friends and family, angels, VCs, and what each wants.
- **High Resolution Fundraising** (Sep 2010) `hiresfund` — Convertible notes let different investors get different prices, which breaks deadlocks and lets rounds close faster.
- **The New Funding Landscape** (Oct 2010) `superangels` — Super-angels and seed funds changed the market. Founders have more leverage, valuations are rising, and series A rounds are shifting.
- **The Future of Startup Funding** (Aug 2010) `future` — Startups need less money, investors who can write small checks win, and the power shifts to founders.
- **Could VC be a Casualty of the Recession?** (Dec 2008) `divergence` — If startups need less money, VCs whose model depends on big checks may get squeezed out.
- **The Venture Capital Squeeze** (Nov 2005) `vcsqueeze` — VCs squeezed from all sides: cheaper startups, angels, and founders who want control.
- **A Unified Theory of VC Suckage** (Mar 2005) `venturecapital` — VC behavior (slow, controlling, hard to deal with) follows from the size of their funds and their incentives, not from bad people.
- **Founder Control** (Dec 2010) `control` — It's increasingly normal for founders to keep board control after a series A. Ask for it.
- ★ **The Equity Equation** (Jul 2007) `equity` — A trade of stock is good if it makes the company more than 1/(1−n) as valuable, where n is the fraction you give up. This applies to investors, hires, and deals.
- **Don't Talk to Corp Dev** (Jan 2015) `corpdev` — Corp dev's job is to buy you. Only talk to them if you want to sell now. Otherwise acquisition talks are a costly distraction.
- **Startup Investing Trends** (Jun 2013) `invtrend` — Returns are dominated by outliers. Valuations and convertible notes are trends. The best founders get to dictate terms.
- **How to Be an Angel Investor** (Mar 2009) `angelinvesting` — The investor's view. Founders matter most, the best deals look risky, and returns follow a power law. Useful for understanding how investors think.
- **Why There Aren't More Googles** (Apr 2008) `googles` — It's mostly not because startups sell too early. VCs push for big rounds and control; founders should resist being pushed into a bad fit.
- **The Pooled-Risk Company Management Company** (Jul 2008) `prcmc` — A thought experiment on a lifestyle business (DHH's argument). Spreading risk across many companies looks a lot like YC.
- **Subject: Airbnb** (Mar 2011) `airbnb` — PG's emails trying to get Fred Wilson to invest. Even great investors miss great companies because the ideas look bad.
- **Black Swan Farming** (Sep 2012) `swan` — Nearly all returns come from a few huge winners, and the best ideas look bad at first. Consensus is therefore a warning sign.

## 5. YC, startup hubs, and the ecosystem

- **A New Venture Animal** (Mar 2008) `ycombinator` — YC is more than seed funding: batches, advice, community, and a new kind of investor that is closer to founders.
- **How Y Combinator Started** (Mar 2012) `ycstart` — YC began as an experiment with Jessica, Robert, and Trevor. They started small and cheap, learned by doing, and stumbled into the batch model.
- **Why YC** (Mar 2006) `whyyc` — PG started YC to work with friends again and to fix how startups were funded. He made the thing he wished had existed.
- **What I Did this Summer** (Oct 2005) `sfp` — Lessons from the first batch: young founders are fine, determination matters most, ideas change, and founders help each other.
- ★ **Why Startup Hubs Work** (Oct 2011) `hubs` — Hubs work because they give you colleagues who expect you to succeed, chance meetings, and an environment where startups are normal.
- **Why to Move to a Startup Hub** (Oct 2007) `startuphubs` — Startups do better in places like Silicon Valley: investors, talent, and a culture that treats startups as normal.
- ★ **Cities and Ambition** (May 2008) `cities` — Every city sends a message: New York says get rich, Cambridge says be smart, Silicon Valley says be powerful (have an effect on the world). Go where the message fits your ambition.
- **How to Be Silicon Valley** (May 2006) `siliconvalley` — You need rich people and nerds. More specifically: a great university, investors, and a place smart people want to live. Government can't just make one.
- **Why Startups Condense in America** (May 2006) `america` — America's advantages: tolerance of failure, immigration, an attitude to wealth, a big market, few rules, and great universities.
- **Can You Buy a Silicon Valley? Maybe.** (Feb 2009) `maybe` — A city could try to shortcut it by funding many startups, with strong conditions. It's expensive and uncertain.
- **How to Make Pittsburgh a Startup Hub** (Apr 2016) `pgh` — Start from a city's real strengths (universities, affordable and livable neighborhoods, weird charm). Don't copy the Valley; make it a place young builders want to live.
- **A Local Revolution?** (Apr 2009) `revolution` — Startups may be a new economic phase, like the industrial revolution, and it might be local to a few places.
- **Where to See Silicon Valley** (Oct 2010) `seesv` — The subtle signs of the Valley: Stanford, garages, the offices of startups and big tech companies.
- **The Founder Visa** (Apr 2009) `foundervisa` — The single cheapest way to get more startups in the US: a visa for founders.
- **Let the Other 95% of Great Programmers In** (Dec 2014) `95` — Great programmers are rare and mostly born outside the US. Immigration limits cost a great deal.
- **The Power of the Marginal** (Jun 2006) `marginal` — Outsiders have advantages: nothing to lose, freedom from what insiders think is proper, and willingness to work on unglamorous things. Insiders are held back by prestige and bureaucracy.
- **The High-Res Society** (Dec 2008) `highres` — Societies are shifting from large organizations to many small units (startups, freelancers). Smaller units can respond to the world faster.
- **After Credentials** (Dec 2008) `credentials` — Credentials matter less as organizations get smaller and can judge actual work. Get good, not certified.

## 6. Wealth and economics

- ★ **How to Make Wealth** (May 2004) `wealth` — Wealth is not money, and it isn't zero-sum; it is stuff people want, and you create it. A startup compresses a working lifetime into a few years. You need leverage and measurement (small groups whose performance can be measured).
- ★ **How People Get Rich Now** (Apr 2021) `richnow` — The Forbes 400 shifted from heirs to founders. The main reason is that startups now let you create wealth fast, because of technology and cheaper starts.
- **Economic Inequality** (Jan 2016) `ineq` — Inequality has many causes; some are bad (poverty), some are good (founders creating wealth). Attack poverty rather than inequality per se.
- **Inequality and Risk** (Aug 2005) `inequality` — Penalizing the upside of risk discourages the risk that creates wealth.
- **Mind the Gap** (May 2004) `gap` — The gap in productivity between the best and the rest is big and growing, which is why technology widens income gaps.
- **Modeling a Wealth Tax** (Aug 2020) `wtax` — Over a founder's lifetime, even small annual wealth taxes compound and consume most of the stock.
- **How to Convert Between Wealth and Income Tax** (May 2026) `winc` — Divide by the risk-free return (~5%): a 1% wealth tax is about equal to a 20% income tax.
- **An Alternative Theory of Unions** (May 2007) `unions` — High-paying union jobs were really a share of corporate monopoly profits, not something workers can easily recreate.
- **Defining Property** (Mar 2012) `property` — Property is a social construct that shifts with technology (for example, music after the internet).
- **Are Software Patents Evil?** (Mar 2006) `softwarepatents` — Startups should mostly ignore patents. Big companies use them as weapons; the best defense is to be good.
- **6,631,372** (Mar 2006) `6631327` — PG learned he'd been granted a patent for an idea (Revenue Loop) he'd built at Viaweb. Patents often don't reflect who actually innovated.
- **The Patent Pledge** (Aug 2011) `patentpledge` — Companies should pledge not to sue small companies with patents first.
- **What the Bubble Got Right** (Sep 2004) `bubble` — The dot-com bubble was wrong about valuations but right about the internet's importance, about startups, and about how fast things would change.
- **Donate Unrestricted** (Mar 2021) `donate` — Give nonprofits money they can use for anything. Restrictions cripple them.
- **An NFT That Saves Lives** (May 2021) `nft` — Noora Health's fundraising NFT: a measurable charity with a new funding mechanism.

## 7. Great work, thinking, and how to live

- ★ **How to Do Great Work** (Jul 2023) `greatwork` — Choose a field you have a natural aptitude for, a deep interest in, and scope for great work. Learn enough to reach the frontier, notice gaps, and pursue them with excited curiosity. Work hard (but not in a way that burns you out), keep going, stay honest, be earnest, and find good colleagues. Work on what you're excited about, not what's prestigious.
- ★ **Superlinear Returns** (Oct 2023) `superlinear` — Returns for performance are often superlinear: through exponential growth (learning, compounding) or thresholds (winner-take-all). Work on things that compound, and seek out learning curves.
- **What to Do** (Mar 2025) `do` — Help people, take care of the world, and make good new things. Making good new things is the best evidence of good thinking. Prefer creating to criticizing; unprestigious work you can show is overlooked is worth seeking out.
- **When To Do What You Love** (Sep 2024) `when` — If you want to get super rich or do great work, work on what interests you most; the best startup ideas come from projects done for fun. When you can't decide, it's usually because you lack information, so try things and "stay upwind." You become like the people you work with.
- **How to Do What You Love** (Jan 2006) `love` — Most people can't do what they love because they haven't figured out what that is. Prestige is a trap. Test: would you do it even if no one paid you? Find out with experimentation.
- **What Doesn't Seem Like Work?** (Jan 2015) `work` — The work that doesn't feel like work to you is a strong clue about what you should do.
- ★ **A Project of One's Own** (Jun 2021) `own` — Working on your own project feels different and produces more. Many great things started as side projects. Encourage that feeling in yourself and in kids.
- ★ **How to Work Hard** (Jun 2021) `hwh` — To do great things you have to work very hard. Find the real problem, work on the hard core of it, use your time well, and judge honestly whether you're working hard enough. Hard work is a skill that needs constant attention.
- **The Bus Ticket Theory of Genius** (Nov 2019) `genius` — Great work comes from ability, determination, and an obsessive, disinterested interest in a particular topic. Cultivate odd obsessions.
- **Beyond Smart** (Oct 2021) `smart` — What's special about great people isn't intelligence but having new, important ideas. Aim to generate ideas, not just to be smart.
